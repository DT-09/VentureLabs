# Sample validation report: reducing last-minute appointment gaps

> **Illustrative example only.** The company, customer statements, numbers, and scenario below are fictional. This document demonstrates report structure; it is not market research, a real customer case study, or evidence of demand.

## Executive decision

**Recommendation: run a narrow discovery test before building software.**

The proposed buyer is an independent dental clinic manager or owner who is responsible for appointment utilization. The hypothesis is that short-notice cancellations create enough recurring operational loss that clinics will consider paying for a better way to refill empty appointment slots.

No evidence in this sample establishes the frequency of the problem, willingness to pay, or the effectiveness of any proposed solution. The next decision should depend on direct workflow evidence and a buying signal—not on the appeal of the idea.

## Decision question

Will a clearly defined segment of independent dental clinics commit time and money to a small pilot that reduces unfilled chair time caused by short-notice cancellations?

## Thesis

- **Target customer:** independent dental clinics with a consistently booked schedule and a front-desk team.
- **Problem hypothesis:** late cancellations create unfilled appointment slots and staff work to refill them.
- **Current workaround hypothesis:** staff make manual calls or use an informal short-notice list.
- **Economic consequence to measure:** unfilled chair time, staff time spent contacting patients, and any rescheduling friction.
- **Buyer hypothesis:** clinic owner or manager can approve a small operational pilot.
- **Key uncertainty:** whether the problem is frequent and costly enough—and whether the proposed workflow is meaningfully better than the current workaround.

## Evidence ledger

| Item | Status | What it supports | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founder belief that cancellations are costly | Assumption | A reason to investigate the workflow | Frequency, loss amount, or willingness to pay |
| A clinic manager describes the last cancellation workflow in detail | To be collected | Current process and context, if directly recorded | General demand across clinics |
| Time-stamped records of cancellation gaps over a defined period | To be collected | Frequency and potential operational impact in that clinic | A generalizable market size |
| A buyer agrees to a paid pilot with written scope | To be collected | A stronger willingness-to-pay signal from that buyer | Repeatability across segments |

No primary evidence has been collected in this illustrative case. All evidence remains unverified until it is actually collected and documented.

## Decision-critical assumptions

1. The problem occurs often enough to matter.
2. Existing workflows fail to refill enough of the affected slots.
3. The clinic can quantify the operational cost.
4. The budget owner considers the problem worth solving.
5. The proposed workflow can improve outcomes without creating excessive staff burden or privacy risk.

## Proposed experiments

### Experiment 1 — Reconstruct the recent workflow

- **Method:** ask a small number of target clinics to walk through recent cancellation examples using non-sensitive, anonymized records.
- **Record:** time between cancellation and appointment, whether the slot was refilled, staff actions, and the clinic's existing tools.
- **Success threshold:** set before interviews begin, based on a defined minimum recurrence and a specific operational cost.
- **Failure threshold:** target clinics report low recurrence, easy resolution, or no meaningful cost.
- **Guardrail:** do not collect identifiable patient information.

### Experiment 2 — Test a concrete pilot offer

- **Method:** present a narrow pilot with defined scope, timeline, operating requirements, and price to qualified budget owners.
- **Record:** whether the buyer accepts, declines, requests a change, or offers a concrete next step.
- **Success threshold:** set in advance; prioritize paid commitment over compliments or general interest.
- **Failure threshold:** repeated lack of commitment after the offer is understood.
- **Guardrail:** do not build the full product before a buyer commits to a specific pilot or another strong buying signal.

## Next actions

1. Select one clinic segment and identify the role that owns the decision.
2. Conduct permissioned workflow discovery without collecting patient-identifiable data.
3. Record source, date, exact observation, and the interpretation separately.
4. Set experiment thresholds before reviewing outcomes.
5. Test a specific pilot offer.
6. Update the recommendation only after evidence is collected.
