# Venture validation report: delivery playbook

## Purpose

Turn a founder's specific commercial decision into a concise, evidence-traceable report. The paid value is disciplined synthesis and a decision-ready next step—not a score, a generic business plan, or a claim that demand has been independently verified.

## Best-fit engagement

A founder or small team has a live decision such as:
- Which customer segment should we test first?
- Is the pain sufficiently frequent and costly to justify a pilot?
- Which assumption is most likely to invalidate this idea?
- What is the smallest experiment that can distinguish interest from buying intent?
- What evidence would justify continuing, changing direction, or stopping?

Avoid taking on vague requests such as “tell me if my startup will succeed.” No report can establish that from a short questionnaire.

## Inputs to request

1. A one-sentence product / service description.
2. One primary target customer and the role that controls the budget.
3. The problem and current workaround.
4. Existing customer conversations, workflow observations, pilot data, or public sources the founder is permitted to share.
5. The current decision and deadline.
6. Known constraints: access to buyers, geography, price range, regulations, and available time.
7. Any claims the founder specifically wants stress-tested.

Ask the client to remove unnecessary personal data and confidential third-party information. Do not request passwords, customer databases, payment details, or information they do not have permission to share.

## Delivery procedure

### 1. Define the decision

Write the decision question in one sentence. Identify what the founder might do differently depending on the answer. If no decision changes, narrow the engagement before starting.

### 2. Separate claims by evidence status

Use these labels consistently:
- **Observed:** a specific event or behavior was directly seen or recorded by the founder.
- **Corroborated:** multiple independent pieces of evidence support the same claim; record what they are.
- **Contradicted:** evidence conflicts with the claim.
- **Unverified:** assertion, interpretation, or source that has not been checked.
- **Assumption:** a belief used to plan the test, not evidence.

A label describes the submitted material and the reviewer's stated basis. Do not imply independent verification unless the relevant source was actually checked and the report records how.

### 3. Identify decision-critical assumptions

For each assumption, document:
- The claim and why the decision depends on it.
- Current evidence and its provenance.
- The strongest plausible alternative explanation.
- What would falsify the claim.
- The lowest-cost experiment that could produce useful evidence.

Prioritize assumptions that could invalidate the business, not the easiest assumptions to test.

### 4. Define an experiment before interpreting results

Each experiment should specify:
- Hypothesis.
- Target population and sample / scope.
- Method and time window.
- Success threshold.
- Failure threshold.
- What result would remain inconclusive.
- Owner and deadline.

Do not change thresholds after seeing the outcome without recording that change. Interest, compliments, waitlist signups, and non-binding survey answers are weaker buying signals than a paid pilot, signed commitment, or observed repeated use.

### 5. Write the decision report

Recommended structure:
1. Executive decision and confidence level.
2. Decision question and scope.
3. Target customer, problem, and current workaround.
4. Evidence ledger with source/date/status.
5. Contradictory evidence and alternative explanations.
6. Critical assumptions ranked by decision impact and uncertainty.
7. Experiments with pre-defined thresholds.
8. Recommendation: continue testing, narrow, pivot, or pause.
9. Next actions, owner, and deadline.
10. Limitations and unresolved questions.

State what is known, what is inferred, and what remains unknown. Do not claim a statistical confidence level unless the method and data support one.

### 6. Quality review before delivery

- Every material claim is traceable to a supplied or actually checked source.
- Hypotheses and founder opinions are not presented as facts.
- Contradictory evidence is included.
- The recommendation follows from the evidence and names its uncertainty.
- Experiments are feasible and falsifiable.
- The report includes explicit next actions and a decision date.
- The founder can correct factual errors before the report is finalized.
- The report says which work was and was not performed.

## Commercial framing

Quote the engagement based on the agreed scope, turnaround, source-checking effort, and complexity. Do not publish or promise a fixed fee by default. Before beginning, agree in writing on the deliverable, deadline, revision boundary, payment method, data handling, and what counts as completion. Do not begin unpaid custom analysis on the assumption that payment will follow.

A useful first paid pilot should be narrow enough to deliver reliably and valuable enough to affect a real decision. Ask for payment for the report itself—not for a promise of startup success.

## Explicit exclusions unless separately agreed and actually performed

- Customer interviews or outreach.
- Independent market sizing or competitor research.
- Legal, tax, investment, or regulatory advice.
- Statistical validation or causal analysis.
- Verification of private claims that cannot be independently checked.
- Guaranteed revenue, funding, product-market fit, or investment outcomes.

## Reusable closing statement

“This report organizes the evidence available for the stated decision and recommends the next test. It does not guarantee commercial success. Claims not supported by the recorded evidence remain assumptions or unknowns.”
